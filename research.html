Research

My dissertation asks whether evolutionary history carries enough information about how species live in communities to reconstruct community structure where direct observation is thin. It runs in three steps: show that the information is there, turn it into predictors, then apply the predictors to communities no one has observed in full.

Is the Information There?

Places more than a million recorded feeding interactions on a dated tree of life and measures how strongly evolutionary history structures trophic roles: how many partners a species has, which partners close relatives share, and how widely those partners spread across the tree. Asks at what taxonomic scale that structure appears, and whether what eats a species is more conserved than what it eats. This work also builds the map of the tree of life that the predictors below read.

Evolutionary history structures trophic roles across the tree of life. (in preparation)

Turning It into Predictors

Develops two machine learning models that read nothing but a species' position on a map of the tree of life. The first scores whether one species eats another and assembles those scores into a food web from a species list alone, validated against held-out interactions, withheld species, and more than a thousand observed local food webs. The second predicts adult body mass, trained on a body-mass corpus harmonized from over 100 sources and published allometric conversions, and validated on families with no measured member. Applied across the tree, it supplies a mass estimate for every described species.

Evolutionary history predicts the structure of local food webs. (in preparation)

Body size is conserved across the tree of life. (in preparation)

Applying the Predictors Where Observation Is Thin

Applies both models to communities that have never been observed in full. Across the National Ecological Observatory Network, builds a food web from the species list at every site and year with one fixed model and asks whether aquatic and terrestrial webs are structured differently, and which differences come from ecology rather than from the method and the data products. On an island metacommunity, reads a predicted diet for every animal consumer in a century of survey records and asks how diet breadth changed as the community reassembled after a severe disturbance.

Effects of Microplastics on Freshwater Fish

Earlier research examined how microplastic exposure influences foraging behavior, swimming performance, escape responses, and body condition in freshwater fish species, contributing to understanding of how emerging contaminants affect aquatic organisms.
